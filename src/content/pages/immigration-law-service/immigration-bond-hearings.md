## What Is an Immigration Bond?

An immigration bond is money paid to the government as a guarantee that a detained person will attend all future immigration hearings and comply with release conditions. It isn't a fine, and it isn't a penalty. If the person attends every hearing and follows the conditions, the bond is generally returned when the case ends, whatever the outcome of the case itself.

Three points often cause confusion:

- **Bond doesn't resolve the immigration case.** It only decides whether the person is detained or free while the case proceeds.
- **Bond is not the same as criminal bail.** It is set under immigration law, by ICE or an immigration judge, and the rules are different.
- **There is a statutory minimum.** Federal law sets a minimum bond amount of $1,500, though many bonds are much higher.

## How Bond Works in Immigration Detention

### Step 1: ICE Makes the First Decision

After arrest, ICE decides whether to keep the person in custody or release them on bond or other conditions. ICE sets the initial amount, if it offers bond at all, and it often sets it high.

### Step 2: Asking the Immigration Judge to Review

If ICE denies release or sets a bond the family can't afford, the detained person can ask an immigration judge to review the decision. This request is commonly called a bond redetermination hearing. The judge hears from both sides and decides whether to grant bond and in what amount.

### Step 3: Paying the Bond

Once a bond is set, someone posts it, typically through an ICE office or an approved online system. The person paying is called the obligor, and they are generally a U.S. citizen, a permanent resident, or someone else who is lawfully present. Choosing the obligor and understanding their responsibilities is an important conversation to have with your attorney. The obligor can lose the money if the detained person misses a hearing.

### Step 4: Release and Conditions

After payment, ICE processes the release. Conditions can include reporting requirements or electronic monitoring. Sometimes processing takes time, so plan for delays.

## What the Judge Looks At

At a bond hearing, the detained person generally has to show they are neither a danger to the community nor a flight risk. Judges look at the whole picture. These are the areas where preparation makes a difference.

### Ties to the Community

Length of residence, family in the area, a stable home, and community involvement all help. Letters from family, employers, faith leaders, and neighbors can carry weight, especially when they are specific.

### Work and Financial Stability

Employment history, tax records, and the ability to support family show stability. If the person is the main earner for children or a spouse, the judge should hear that.

### Criminal History

The judge considers the nature, age, and seriousness of any convictions, along with evidence of rehabilitation. Certain convictions trigger mandatory detention, which is covered below. For others, a thoughtful presentation of the record, sentence completion, and treatment or counseling can matter.

### Immigration History

Prior removal orders, missed hearings, or prior immigration violations can weigh against release. A clear explanation, supported by documents, can help.

### Likelihood of Relief

If the person has a strong claim to stay in the country, such as a pending family petition, asylum application, or cancellation of removal, a judge may view the incentive to appear as stronger.

### Ability to Pay

The bond amount should be set with the person's circumstances in mind. If the proposed amount is out of reach, we can argue for a lower amount, supported by evidence of what the family can realistically pay.

## Who May Not Be Eligible for a Bond Hearing?

This is the most important and fastest-moving part of bond law, so we'll explain it carefully.

### Mandatory Detention Based on Criminal History

Federal law requires detention, without bond, for people who fall into certain criminal categories, including some drug offenses, crimes involving moral turpitude, aggravated felonies, and firearm offenses. Whether a conviction actually triggers this rule depends on the exact statute, the record of conviction, and the sentence. The labels used in state court don't always match immigration law. When the government's classification looks wrong, a person can challenge it at what is often called a *Joseph* hearing.

### Disputes Over People Who Entered Without Inspection

In 2025, the Board of Immigration Appeals issued a decision, *Matter of Yajure Hurtado*, taking the position that many people who entered the United States without inspection are subject to mandatory detention and are not eligible for a bond hearing before an immigration judge. Many federal district courts have disagreed and have ordered bond hearings in individual habeas cases, while at least one federal appeals court has sided with the government's reading. The issue has been working its way through the appellate courts, including in the Ninth Circuit, which covers California.

The practical result is that eligibility can depend on the person's history, where they are detained, and which court is looking at the case. We track the current rulings and tell you where things stand when you call.

### People With Final Orders

People detained after a final removal order are usually in a different legal category, and bond hearings generally aren't available in the same way. Other protections, such as limits on prolonged detention, may apply.

## When the Immigration Judge Won't Hold a Bond Hearing

Sometimes the immigration court says it lacks authority to hear a bond request. That isn't always the end. A federal habeas corpus petition under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 asks a district court to decide whether the detention is lawful and, if appropriate, to order a hearing or release.

Public dockets show this approach working in cases handled by this office:

- ***Lopez-Aldama v. Rios*** **(C.D. Cal., filed August 2026):** The court required the government to provide an individualized bond hearing before an immigration judge within seven days, or release the petitioner on narrowly tailored conditions. Other requested relief was denied.
- ***Correa-Correa v. Albarran*** **(E.D. Cal., filed February 2026):** After an emergency motion, the court ordered immediate release and restricted re-detention without notice and a pre-deprivation hearing before a neutral adjudicator.
- ***Bermudez v. Albarran*** **(E.D. Cal., filed February 2026):** The court ordered release under prior conditions and limited further detention without notice and a hearing.

Every case turns on its own facts, and these results don't guarantee any particular outcome. For more detail, see our page on habeas corpus petitions.

## After the Bond Decision

### If Bond Is Granted

The government can sometimes ask for a stay of the judge's decision or appeal it, which can delay release. We follow the order closely and handle post-hearing steps so the family isn't left guessing.

### If Bond Is Denied or Set Too High

You can appeal a custody decision to the Board of Immigration Appeals. A new bond request can also be filed if circumstances change materially, such as new evidence, a changed family situation, or a long stretch of detention. In some cases, federal court is the next step.

### Keeping the Underlying Case on Track

Bond is only one part. The person still needs a plan for the removal case, whether that means asylum, cancellation of removal, adjustment of status, or another form of relief. Being out of detention makes it much easier to gather evidence and prepare. Our deportation defense page explains the broader process.

## Frequently Asked Questions

#### 1. How much is an immigration bond?

The legal minimum is $1,500, but amounts vary widely and can be much higher. The judge considers danger, flight risk, and the person's circumstances. A lawyer can argue for a lower amount with evidence of what your family can afford.

#### 2. Does everyone in ICE detention get a bond hearing?

No. People held under mandatory detention rules aren't eligible, and the rules for people who entered without inspection have been disputed in the courts. Eligibility depends on the person's history and the current law, which is why an early review matters.

#### 3. How long does it take to get a bond hearing?

Detained hearings are usually scheduled faster than regular cases, often within a few weeks, though timing varies by court and caseload. If the court refuses to hold a hearing, a habeas petition may speed things up.

#### 4. Do I get my money back after paying bond?

Generally yes, if the detained person attends every hearing and follows all release conditions. The bond can be returned even if the immigration case is lost, as long as the conditions were met. Some fees may apply if you use a bond company.

#### 5. What happens if the bond hearing is denied?

You may be able to appeal to the Board of Immigration Appeals, file a new request if circumstances change, or ask a federal court to review the detention. Deadlines are short, so contact an attorney quickly.

#### 6. Where are bond hearings held for people detained near San Francisco?

Bond hearings are held at the immigration court that covers the facility where the person is detained, often by video. That may not be in the Bay Area, since people are frequently transferred.

#### 7. How do I find out where my family member is being held?

Use the ICE Online Detainee Locator with the person's A-number and country of birth, or call us. Transfers happen quickly and the system sometimes lags.

#### 8. I live in Oakland or Daly City. Can I still work with your office?

Yes. Consultations can happen by phone or video, and documents can be shared electronically.

#### 9. Can you represent someone detained outside the Bay Area?

Often yes. Maisoun Sulfab is admitted in the Northern, Eastern, Central, and Southern Districts of California, and bond hearings can be attended remotely in many cases.
