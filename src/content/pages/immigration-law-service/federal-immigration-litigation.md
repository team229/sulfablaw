## What Is Federal Immigration Litigation?

Federal immigration litigation means bringing a case in federal court against the government over an immigration matter. The defendants are typically agencies and officials, such as USCIS, ICE, the Department of Homeland Security, or the Attorney General.

It differs from ordinary immigration work in a few important ways:

- **Different judge.** Cases are heard by federal district judges, not immigration judges or agency officers.
- **Different rules.** Federal court follows the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure and local court rules, with strict filing deadlines and formal briefing.
- **Different goals.** Federal court usually can't hand you a green card directly. It can order the government to act, correct unlawful decisions, or end unlawful detention.

That last point is important. Federal litigation is a tool for specific problems, and it works best when it's matched to the right one.

## Types of Federal Court Immigration Cases

### Mandamus and APA Lawsuits for Unreasonable Delay

Many people call us because their case has stalled. Adjustment of status, naturalization, family petitions, work permits, and asylum interviews can all linger far beyond normal processing times.

An immigration mandamus lawyer can file suit asking a federal court to compel the agency to do what it's legally required to do. These cases typically rely on the mandamus statute, 28 U.S.C. § 1361, and the Administrative Procedure Act, which allows courts to compel agency action that has been unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.

Courts don't treat every wait as unreasonable. They generally look at how long the delay has been, whether Congress set a timeline, the effect on health and welfare, and competing agency priorities. The facts of your case matter more than the calendar alone.

In *Dougaz v. USCIS*, a federal case in which Maisoun Sulfab represented the plaintiff, the court approved a stipulation in January 2026 keeping the case on hold while the parties pursued an administrative resolution. That's a common path in delay cases: the lawsuit creates a reason for the agency to decide, and the court case pauses or ends once it does. Every case is different, and no filing guarantees that result.

### Naturalization Delays and Denials

If USCIS hasn't decided your naturalization application within 120 days after your interview, federal law allows you to ask a district court to take over the application. If USCIS denied your application and you've completed the administrative appeal, you can seek review in federal court. Both paths have deadlines and strategic tradeoffs worth discussing before filing.

### Challenging Unlawful Denials

Sometimes the problem isn't delay, it's the decision. The APA lets federal courts set aside final agency actions that are arbitrary, contrary to law, or unsupported by the record. This can apply to denials of family petitions, adjustment applications, and other benefits. Judges generally won't second-guess discretionary decisions, and some statutes limit court review, so the first question is always whether the court has authority to hear the claim.

### Immigration Habeas Corpus Petitions

When a person is held in immigration detention, a habeas corpus petition under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 asks a federal judge to decide whether the detention is lawful. Typical claims include denial of a bond hearing, prolonged detention, re-detention without notice, and detention when removal isn't reasonably foreseeable. Our habeas corpus petitions page covers this in more detail.

### Petitions for Review of Board of Immigration Appeals Decisions

After the Board of Immigration Appeals denies an appeal in a removal case, you may be able to ask a federal court of appeals, generally the Ninth Circuit for California, to review the decision. The deadline is short, generally 30 days from the Board's final order, and the court's review is limited to legal and constitutional questions in many cases. If you've just lost an appeal, don't wait.

### Related Federal Claims

Other matters can also end up in federal court, including FOIA lawsuits to obtain immigration records, constitutional claims, and declaratory relief. Whether they make sense depends on the facts.

## Is Federal Court the Right Move?

Filing in federal court isn't always the first step, and it isn't always the best one. We usually look at these questions.

### Have Administrative Options Been Tried?

Courts often expect you to use available administrative remedies first, such as inquiries, expedite requests, appeals, or motions. Sometimes those remedies are exhausted, unavailable, or too slow. Sometimes they work, and a lawsuit isn't necessary.

### Does the Court Have Jurisdiction?

Immigration statutes limit when federal courts can step in, particularly for discretionary decisions and for certain removal-related issues. Identifying a claim the court can actually hear is the first hurdle.

### Is There a Clear Legal Duty or Violation?

Mandamus and APA claims depend on showing the agency has a legal obligation it hasn't met, or has acted unlawfully. Habeas claims depend on showing detention violates the law or the Constitution.

### What Are the Costs and Risks?

Federal litigation involves court filing fees, service on government officials, briefing, and sometimes hearings. It can also draw attention to your case. We talk through realistic benefits and downsides before filing, so you can decide with full information.

## How a Federal Immigration Case Proceeds

### Step 1: Case Review and Strategy

We review your file, timeline, agency correspondence, and prior filings, then identify the claim, the proper defendants, and the right court.

### Step 2: Pre-Suit Efforts

In many delay cases, we first send a demand letter or contact agency counsel. Sometimes the agency acts without a lawsuit.

### Step 3: Filing the Complaint or Petition

If we file, the complaint or petition sets out the facts, the legal basis, and the relief requested. In urgent matters, such as detention, we may file an emergency motion for a temporary restraining order or preliminary injunction at the same time.

### Step 4: Service and the Government's Response

The government attorneys, usually from the U.S. Attorney's Office, respond to the filing. Many cases resolve at this stage. The agency may adjudicate the application, schedule an interview, hold a bond hearing, or agree to a stipulated resolution.

### Step 5: Briefing, Hearing, and Decision

If the case doesn't resolve, the court sets a briefing schedule, may hold a hearing, and issues a ruling. Outcomes range from an order compelling action to dismissal.

### Step 6: After the Ruling

A favorable ruling usually returns the matter to the agency with instructions. We stay involved until the agency actually acts. If the ruling goes the other way, we discuss appeal options and deadlines.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Service-Related Questions

#### 1. What is a mandamus lawsuit in immigration cases?

It's a federal lawsuit asking a court to order a government agency to perform a duty it hasn't carried out, often to decide an application that has been delayed. It doesn't tell the agency to approve your case, only to make a decision.

#### 2. How long does a federal immigration lawsuit take?

It varies. Some delay cases resolve within weeks of filing when the agency acts. Habeas cases involving detention can move faster. Cases that go through full briefing can take several months or longer.

#### 3. Will filing a lawsuit hurt my immigration case?

There's no automatic penalty for suing, but any filing has strategic consequences. The agency may re-examine your file, so we review your history for risks first. That's one reason a careful case review comes before filing.

#### 4. Can a federal court approve my green card or citizenship?

Usually it can't grant the benefit itself, apart from certain naturalization situations. It can order the agency to decide your case, correct unlawful decisions, or send the matter back for a proper review.

#### 5. What are the costs of a federal immigration case?

Costs include court filing fees and attorney fees, and they vary with complexity and whether emergency motions are needed. We explain the fee structure during your consultation, before anything is filed.
