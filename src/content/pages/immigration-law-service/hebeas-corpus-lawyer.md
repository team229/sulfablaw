## What Is an Immigration Habeas Corpus Petition?

Habeas corpus is a Latin phrase usually translated as "you shall have the body." In practice, it is a petition filed in federal district court asking a judge to decide whether a person's detention is lawful. The government has to respond in writing and identify the legal authority it relies on to hold the person.

Two points are worth understanding right away.

- **It is a federal court case, not an immigration court case.** An immigration judge decides whether someone can stay in the United States. A federal judge decides whether the government can keep that person locked up while the process plays out.
- **It challenges the detention, not the underlying immigration case.** A successful petition can lead to release or a bond hearing, but it doesn't by itself grant a green card or end a removal case. Those matters continue separately.

The usual legal basis is 28 U.S.C. § 2241, which lets people in custody challenge the legality of that custody in federal court, along with the Due Process Clause of the Fifth Amendment.

## When a Habeas Corpus Attorney in California Can Help

Not every detention is unlawful, and a habeas petition isn't the right tool for every case. These are the situations where federal court review most often comes up.

### Detention Without a Bond Hearing

Many people held under immigration law are entitled to an individualized bond hearing before an immigration judge. When the government treats someone as subject to mandatory detention, or simply doesn't schedule a hearing, a habeas petition can ask the court to order one. In one recent Central District of California case handled by this office, the court ordered the government to provide a bond hearing within seven days or release the petitioner on tailored conditions.

### Prolonged Detention

Detention that drags on without meaningful review raises due process concerns. Courts look at how long the person has been held, whether the case is moving, and whether any end point is in sight. There is no single magic number of days, which is why the facts of each case matter so much.

### Re-Detention After Release

Some clients have been released, lived under conditions for months or years, and then been arrested again without warning. Habeas petitions can challenge re-detention that happens without notice and without a hearing before a neutral decision-maker.

### Removal Not Reasonably Foreseeable

When someone has a final removal order but the government can't realistically carry it out, for example because the home country won't issue travel documents, continued detention can become unlawful. The Supreme Court addressed this in *Zadvydas v. Davis*, and federal courts still apply that reasoning today.

### The Wrong Detention Category

Immigration detention rests on different statutes depending on a person's history: arrival at the border, prior orders, or a criminal record. If the government is applying the wrong one, the consequences are large. A careful review often finds the answer sits in a few pages of a file that nobody has looked at closely.

## How the Federal Habeas Process Works

### Step 1: Case Review

Everything starts with the facts. We look at the arrest, the detention paperwork, prior immigration filings, any criminal history, and what the government has said about why the person is held. Then we decide whether a petition has a real legal basis. If it doesn't, we say so, and we talk about other options such as a bond request or a motion before the immigration judge. Filing a weak petition can waste time you don't have.

### Step 2: Filing the Petition and Emergency Motion

If the case supports it, we file the petition in the proper federal district court. When time matters, we usually file an emergency motion at the same time.

#### Temporary Restraining Order vs. Preliminary Injunction

A temporary restraining order (TRO) is short-term relief meant to preserve the situation while the court looks at the case. A preliminary injunction lasts longer and follows a fuller review. In *Correa-Correa v. Albarran*, filed in the Eastern District of California, the judge converted an emergency motion into a motion for a preliminary injunction, granted it, and ordered immediate release.

### Step 3: The Government's Response

The government must answer in writing and explain its authority for the detention. Sometimes this leads to a bond hearing or release without a ruling. Sometimes the government fights, and the judge decides on the written arguments or after a hearing.

### Step 4: The Court's Decision

A judge may grant the petition, grant partial relief, or deny it. Partial relief is more common than people expect. It might mean a bond hearing within a set number of days rather than outright release. After a ruling, we explain what it means, what the government must do next, and how it fits with the rest of the immigration case.

## Documented Results in Federal Habeas Cases

The outcomes below come from public court dockets. Every case turns on its own facts, and past results don't predict future ones. They do show what federal courts have been willing to order.

- ***Ricardo Correa-Correa v. Albarran*** **(E.D. Cal., filed February 2026):** Habeas petition and emergency motion. The court ordered immediate release and barred re-detention without notice and a pre-deprivation hearing before a neutral adjudicator, subject to exigent circumstances.
- ***Maurice Nganda-Mekumba v. Albarran*** **(E.D. Cal., filed February 2026):** The court first ordered release, then granted the habeas petition in June 2026 and ordered that he not be re-detained without constitutional protections. The case closed after the order.
- ***Qusay Charaf v. Rios*** **(C.D. Cal., filed April 2026):** The court granted the petition in May 2026 and ordered immediate release, subject to the conditions of his existing parole.
- ***Emilio Bertis Bermudez v. Albarran*** **(E.D. Cal., filed February 2026):** The court granted emergency relief, ordering release under the same conditions that applied before re-detention, and restricted further detention without notice and a pre-detention hearing.
- ***Victor Hugo Lopez-Aldama v. Rios*** **(C.D. Cal., filed August 2026):** Partial relief. The court required an individualized bond hearing within seven days, or release on narrow conditions, and denied other requested relief.

That last case is worth noticing. Not every petition ends in release, and a bond hearing can be a meaningful win when someone has been held without one.

## Habeas Corpus, Bond Hearings, and Appeals: Which Fits Your Situation?

These tools are often confused, so here is a plain comparison.

- **Bond hearing request:** Asked of an immigration judge. It is usually the first step when a person is eligible for bond.
- **Immigration appeal:** Challenges a decision in the removal case itself, such as a denial of relief. It doesn't directly address detention.
- **Habeas corpus petition:** Goes to federal court when detention itself appears unlawful, unreasonably prolonged, or imposed without required process.

Often these run side by side. A person may be pursuing asylum or defending against removal in immigration court while a habeas petition addresses the custody question in federal court.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Service-Related Questions

#### 1. What is the difference between an immigration habeas corpus lawyer and a regular immigration lawyer?

Many immigration lawyers focus on applications and immigration court. A habeas corpus attorney also litigates in federal district court, challenging the legality of detention itself. The two roles often overlap, and the same attorney may handle the immigration case and the federal petition.

#### 2. How long does a habeas corpus petition take?

It varies. Emergency motions can produce an order within days or a couple of weeks. A full petition can take weeks to months, depending on the court's schedule and how the government responds. Sometimes the government agrees to a bond hearing or release after the petition is filed.

#### 3. Will a habeas petition get my family member released?

No lawyer can promise that. Outcomes depend on the legal basis for detention, the person's history, and the judge. Possible results include release, a bond hearing within a set deadline, or denial. A frank case review is the best way to understand the odds.

#### 4. Can I file for habeas corpus if my family member has a final removal order?

Possibly. If removal isn't reasonably foreseeable, or if detention has become unreasonably long, a petition may still be an option. A habeas petition doesn't usually stop removal on its own, so the specific facts and any emergency relief matter a great deal.

#### 5. What does it cost to file a federal habeas corpus petition?

Fees depend on the complexity and whether an emergency motion is needed. We explain costs during the consultation so you can decide with full information before anything is filed.
