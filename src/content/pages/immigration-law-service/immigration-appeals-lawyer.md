## What Is an Immigration Appeal?

An immigration appeal asks a higher body to review a decision made by an immigration judge, USCIS, or another agency official. The reviewing body doesn't start the case over. It looks at the record and decides whether the original decision was legally and factually sound.

That has two practical consequences:

- **The record is everything.** What was said and submitted at the earlier stage is generally what the appeal is based on. New evidence usually belongs in a different filing, such as a motion to reopen.
- **Arguments must be specific.** A general claim that the decision was unfair won't carry the day. The appeal has to identify particular errors of law or fact.

## Appealing to the Board of Immigration Appeals

The Board of Immigration Appeals (BIA) is the highest administrative body for interpreting immigration law. If an immigration judge rules against you, the BIA is generally your next stop.

### The 30-Day Deadline

A notice of appeal usually must be filed within 30 days of the immigration judge's decision. That deadline is strict. If you miss it, the judge's ruling typically becomes final. If the decision was delivered orally in court, the clock starts that day, which can catch people off guard.

### What the Notice of Appeal Must Include

The notice should state the reasons for the appeal in enough detail for the Board to understand the claimed errors. A vague notice can lead to summary dismissal. We prepare it with the legal grounds clearly laid out, and we preserve arguments that may matter later.

### The Briefing Schedule

After your appeal is accepted, the BIA sets a briefing schedule. This is the written argument stage. A strong brief identifies each error, cites the record, and explains why the law requires a different result. Extensions are limited, so we plan ahead.

### How the Board Reviews Your Case

The BIA reviews the record, including the transcript and exhibits. Generally, it looks at legal questions fresh, but it gives deference to an immigration judge's factual findings, overturning them only for clear error. That standard shapes our strategy. Legal errors are often more promising than disagreements about facts.

### What Happens While the Appeal Is Pending

In most cases, a timely appeal pauses the removal order for a person who isn't detained. If you are detained, the appeal doesn't automatically mean release. Detention and bond are separate questions. See our page on immigration bond hearings for how those work.

### Possible Outcomes

The Board can dismiss the appeal, sustain it, or send the case back to the immigration judge for further proceedings, which is called a remand. A remand isn't a final win, but it can give you a second chance to present evidence or have a legal issue reconsidered.

## Motions to Reopen and Motions to Reconsider

Sometimes the right step isn't an appeal, or it's an appeal plus something else. Two motions often come up.

### Motion to Reopen

A motion to reopen asks the judge or Board to reopen a closed case because of new, material evidence that wasn't available at the earlier hearing. Common grounds include:

- New facts or changed circumstances, such as a new immigration option or changed country conditions
- A family relationship or eligibility for relief that wasn't available before
- Ineffective assistance of prior counsel, where specific procedural requirements apply
- Lack of proper notice of a hearing

There are time limits, often 90 days from the final order, and a limit on how many motions can be filed. Some situations carry different timelines or exceptions, so a case-specific review is essential.

### Motion to Reopen After an In Absentia Order

If you missed a hearing and were ordered removed in your absence, you may be able to reopen the case. The rules depend on the reason you missed the hearing. If you never received proper notice, there's generally no deadline, though acting quickly is still wise. If exceptional circumstances kept you away, such as serious illness, the time limit is much shorter.

### Motion to Reconsider

A motion to reconsider asks the decision-maker to take another look at the same record because of a legal or factual error. It generally must be filed within 30 days, and you get one. It doesn't introduce new evidence.

### Stays of Removal

Filing a motion to reopen or reconsider doesn't always stop removal on its own. When removal is imminent, we may also request a stay. That can be urgent, so don't wait until the last week.

## Appeals After the Board: Federal Court Review

If the BIA rules against you, there may be further review in federal court.

### Petition for Review in the Court of Appeals

You can generally file a petition for review with the federal court of appeals, which for California is the Ninth Circuit. The deadline is generally 30 days from the BIA's final order, and it isn't extendable. Federal law limits what the court can review, particularly for people with certain criminal convictions and for discretionary decisions, but legal and constitutional questions can often still be raised. A petition for review usually doesn't stop removal automatically, so a separate request for a stay may be needed.

### Habeas Corpus and Other Federal Actions

For detention problems, a federal habeas corpus petition may be available in district court. Public dockets show federal courts in California granting relief in habeas cases handled by this office, including *Correa-Correa v. Albarran* (E.D. Cal., 2026), where the court ordered release, and *Lopez-Aldama v. Rios* (C.D. Cal., 2026), where the court required a bond hearing within seven days or release on conditions. These are detention matters, not appeals, and each case depends on its own facts. See our habeas corpus petitions and federal immigration litigation pages for more.

## Appealing Other Immigration Decisions

Not every appeal comes from immigration court.

- **USCIS denials.** Some denials, such as certain employment-based petitions and waivers, can be appealed to the USCIS Administrative Appeals Office, usually within about 30 days. Others can be challenged through a motion to reopen or reconsider with USCIS.
- **Family petition denials.** Certain denied family-based petitions go to the Board of Immigration Appeals.
- **Delays and unlawful denials.** If an agency has delayed a decision unreasonably or acted unlawfully, a federal lawsuit may be an option.

Because each type has its own deadline and forum, the first step is identifying exactly what decision you received and who issued it.

## Is an Appeal Worth Pursuing?

An honest answer depends on the facts. These are some questions we work through with you.

### Was There a Legal Error?

Wrong legal standard, failure to consider key evidence, or violations of procedure can all support an appeal.

### Was the Issue Preserved?

Arguments not raised earlier can be hard to raise later. We review the transcript to see what was and wasn't preserved.

### Is There a Better Route?

Sometimes new evidence or a new legal option makes a motion to reopen more effective than an appeal. In other cases, it's both.

### What Are the Risks and Costs?

Appeals take time and money, and a loss may leave fewer options. We give you a realistic view, including what might happen if you do nothing.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Service-Related Questions

#### 1. How long do I have to appeal an immigration judge's decision?

Generally 30 days from the date of the decision. If the judge ruled orally at your hearing, the clock usually starts that day. Because the deadline is strict, contact an attorney immediately.

#### 2. What is the difference between an appeal and a motion to reopen?

An appeal argues that the decision was wrong based on the existing record. A motion to reopen asks to reopen the case because of new evidence or circumstances. Some cases call for one, some for the other, and some for both.

#### 3. Can I be deported while my appeal is pending?

In most cases, a timely appeal to the BIA pauses the removal order if you aren't detained. Other filings, such as motions or petitions for review, may not stop removal automatically. A stay request may be needed.

#### 4. How long does a BIA appeal take?

It varies. Detained cases often move faster. Non-detained appeals can take many months or longer, depending on the Board's workload. We can give you a better estimate once we've reviewed your file.

#### 5. What are my chances of winning an appeal?

No one can promise an outcome. The strength of an appeal depends on whether there was a legal error, how well the issues were preserved, and the facts. A candid review is the best way to understand your options.

### Location-Related Questions

#### 6. Where do I file a BIA appeal if my hearing was in San Francisco?

BIA appeals are filed with the Board itself, not with the local court, using the procedures in the immigration judge's decision and the Board's rules. We handle the filing and track deadlines.

#### 7. I live in Oakland or Daly City. Do I have to come to San Francisco?

Not necessarily. Most consultations happen by phone or video, and filings can be prepared remotely.

#### 8. Can you help if my family member is detained outside the Bay Area?

Often yes. Appeals are handled on the record, so location is less of a barrier than for hearings. Call us with the person's name and A-number.

#### 9. Does a local immigration court decision get appealed to a local court?

No. Appeals from immigration judges go to the BIA, which is in Virginia. Only after the BIA rules can the case go to the federal court of appeals, which for California is the Ninth Circuit.
