## Who Is Eligible to Become a U.S. Citizen?

Naturalization is the process by which a lawful permanent resident becomes a U.S. citizen. The basic requirements are straightforward on paper. Applying them to your own life is where people get stuck.

### The Five-Year and Three-Year Rules

Most green card holders can apply after five years as a permanent resident. If you're married to a U.S. citizen, have been living with that spouse, and your spouse has held citizenship for the full period, you may be able to apply after three years.

#### Continuous Residence and Physical Presence

You generally must show that you've lived continuously in the United States during the statutory period and that you were physically present for at least half of it. A trip outside the country of more than six months can raise questions. An absence of a year or more can break continuous residence altogether.

This is one of the most common problem areas I see. People who work abroad, care for family overseas, or travel often for business need a careful look at their travel history before filing.

#### Time in Your State

You must also have lived for at least three months in the state or USCIS district where you file.

### Good Moral Character

USCIS looks at your conduct during the statutory period, and sometimes before it. Criminal history, unpaid taxes, failure to pay child support, false statements, and certain other issues can all affect your case.

#### Prior Arrests and Convictions

This is where having an attorney matters most. A past arrest or conviction does not automatically bar you from citizenship, but some offenses can. Filing without understanding how an offense is treated under immigration law can bring your status under review and, in serious cases, lead to removal proceedings.

I'll look at your records before you file, not after. If your situation is risky, I'll tell you that plainly and walk you through your options.

### English and Civics Requirements

Most applicants must show they can read, write, and speak basic English and pass a civics test on U.S. history and government. USCIS has updated the civics test in recent years, and the version you take depends on when you filed, so check the current requirements or ask me.

Some applicants qualify for age-and-residence exemptions from the English requirement, and applicants with qualifying medical conditions may request a disability exception with the proper form.

## The N-400 Application Process in California

If you're looking for an N-400 application lawyer in California, here is what the process typically looks like. Timing varies by field office and caseload.

### Step 1: Eligibility Review

We go through your green card date, travel history, addresses, employment, marriages, children, taxes, and any encounters with law enforcement.

### Step 2: Preparing and Filing the N-400

The N-400 asks for a long list of details going back years. Answers need to match your other records, including past immigration filings. Inconsistencies, even innocent ones, can trigger questions at the interview.

### Step 3: Biometrics Appointment

USCIS usually schedules fingerprinting after your application is accepted.

### Step 4: The Interview and Tests

An officer reviews your application under oath, asks about your background, and gives the English and civics tests. I prepare my clients for the kinds of questions that come up, including the uncomfortable ones.

### Step 5: The Decision and Oath Ceremony

USCIS may approve, continue your case for more evidence, or deny it. If approved, you attend an oath ceremony. You become a citizen when you take the oath, not when you receive the approval notice.

A denial is not always the end. If your N-400 is denied, you may request a hearing with an officer using Form N-336. The deadline is short, generally 30 days from the decision, so don't wait. I can review the denial, explain what went wrong, and help you decide whether to request a hearing, refile, or pursue another option.

## Do You Already Have U.S. Citizenship Through a Parent?

Some people are already citizens and don't know it. If one of your parents or grandparents was a U.S. citizen, you may have acquired or derived citizenship automatically. The rules depend on when you were born, where, and your parents' status at that time.

If that sounds like your family, I can research the question with you. If you qualify, you may be able to apply for a Certificate of Citizenship or a U.S. passport instead of going through naturalization.

Processing times vary by USCIS field office and over time. USCIS publishes estimated processing times online, and I can tell you what to expect for your case.

USCIS generally gives you a second chance to retake the part you failed, usually within 60 to 90 days. I can help you prepare in the meantime.

## About U.S. Citizenship and Naturalization Law

Naturalization is governed by the Immigration and Nationality Act and USCIS regulations. A lawful permanent resident files Form N-400 and must meet requirements for residence, physical presence, good moral character, English, and civics knowledge, as well as attachment to the principles of the Constitution. Denied applicants may request review on Form N-336, and in some cases may seek review in federal district court. Individuals who acquired or derived citizenship through a parent may file Form N-600. Related immigration matters include green card renewals, removal defense, bond hearings, and habeas corpus petitions challenging immigration detention.
