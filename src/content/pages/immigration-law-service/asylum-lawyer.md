## What Is Asylum?

Asylum is a form of protection for people who are already in the United States, or who arrive at a port of entry, and who have been persecuted or fear persecution in their home country. To qualify, you generally need to show that the harm you suffered or fear is connected to one of five protected grounds:

- Race
- Religion
- Nationality
- Membership in a particular social group
- Political opinion

If asylum is granted, you can remain in the United States, apply for work authorization, and, after a waiting period, apply for a green card. In many cases, you can also petition to bring a spouse and unmarried children under 21 to join you.

### Persecution Versus General Hardship

This is where many people misjudge their own case. Asylum doesn't cover every difficult situation. Poverty, general crime, or civil unrest alone usually aren't enough. What matters is whether the harm was serious, whether it was connected to a protected ground, and whether your government was involved or unable or unwilling to protect you. Sometimes a story that sounds like "just crime" turns out to include a motive that fits the law. A careful interview is how we find out.

## Do You Qualify? Key Elements of an Asylum Claim

### Past Persecution or a Well-Founded Fear

You can qualify by showing you were persecuted in the past, by showing a reasonable possibility you will be persecuted in the future, or both. Proof of past persecution creates a presumption that the fear of future harm is well-founded, though the government can try to rebut it by showing conditions have changed.

### A Connection to a Protected Ground

You must show the persecutor was motivated, at least in part, by your race, religion, nationality, political opinion, or membership in a particular social group. Social group claims are among the most complex. They can involve family ties, gender-based harm, sexual orientation or gender identity, or other shared characteristics, and the law in this area keeps developing.

### Government Involvement or Failure to Protect

The persecutor may be a government actor or someone the government can't or won't control, such as a gang, a militia, or a family member. Where the government itself is the source of harm, it can be easier to show you couldn't safely relocate within the country. In other cases, we look at whether moving elsewhere in the country is realistic.

### The One-Year Filing Deadline

Asylum applications generally must be filed within one year of your last arrival in the United States. There are exceptions for changed circumstances or extraordinary circumstances that delayed filing, but those exceptions need to be proven. If you've been in the country for a while, talk to an attorney soon. Time spent waiting can limit your options.

### Bars to Asylum

Certain circumstances can prevent a grant of asylum, such as a serious criminal conviction, prior firm resettlement in another country, or participation in persecuting others. If any of these might apply, a lawyer can assess how they affect your case and whether other protection, such as withholding of removal, may still be available.

## Two Paths to Asylum: Affirmative and Defensive

How you apply depends on whether you're in removal proceedings.

### Affirmative Asylum

If you are not in removal proceedings, you generally file Form I-589 with USCIS and attend an interview with an asylum officer. The officer decides whether to grant the claim or refer the case to immigration court.

### Defensive Asylum in Immigration Court

If you're in removal proceedings, you present your asylum claim to an immigration judge as a defense against removal. This path involves testimony, evidence submissions, and often cross-examination by a government attorney. If you're facing court, see our page on deportation defense for how the whole process works.

### Claims Made at the Border or Port of Entry

People who arrive at a port of entry and ask for protection may go through a credible fear screening before their case proceeds. The rules for this stage change with policy and court rulings, and the stakes are high, so speaking with an attorney as early as possible is wise.

## Building an Asylum Case That Holds Up

An asylum case is mostly a story supported by evidence. The judge or officer has to believe it, and belief depends on detail, consistency, and corroboration.

### Your Personal Declaration

This is your account in your own words: what happened, when, who was involved, and why you believe it was connected to a protected ground. We work with you to make it complete and organized, without embellishment. Inconsistencies, even small ones, can be used against you, so accuracy matters more than drama.

### Supporting Evidence

Common evidence includes:

- Identity and nationality documents
- Police reports, threats, or messages, where available
- Medical or psychological evaluations
- Photographs, news reports, and country conditions reports
- Affidavits from family, friends, or witnesses
- Membership records for political, religious, or community organizations

If documents are hard to obtain, we discuss alternatives and how to explain why they're missing.

### Preparing for the Interview or Hearing

Testifying about trauma isn't easy. Preparation involves walking through your declaration, practicing how questions are asked, and learning the process so nothing surprises you. We also prepare you for the difficult parts, including cross-examination in court.

## Related Protection Beyond Asylum

Sometimes asylum isn't available, for example because of the filing deadline. Other forms of protection may still apply:

- **Withholding of removal.** Requires a higher showing of likelihood of persecution, but has no one-year deadline.
- **Protection under the Convention Against Torture (CAT).** For people likely to face torture by or with the consent of a government official, regardless of protected ground.
- **U and T visas.** For victims of certain crimes or human trafficking who cooperate with law enforcement.
- **Other humanitarian or family-based options.** Sometimes another route fits better than a protection claim.

These options differ in what they offer. Withholding and CAT, for instance, don't lead directly to a green card the way asylum can. We help you compare them.

## After Asylum Is Granted

A grant is a major step, not the last one. Asylees can typically:

- Work lawfully in the United States
- Apply for a green card after being in asylee status for a period set by law
- Petition for a spouse and unmarried children under 21, within the required timeline
- Eventually apply for citizenship once other requirements are met

If you receive a denial or referral to court instead, there may still be next steps. Appeals and motions have strict deadlines, so contact an attorney quickly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Service-Related Questions

#### 1. How long does an asylum case take?

It depends on where you file and on current backlogs. Affirmative cases with USCIS and court cases both can take months or years. Detained cases usually move faster. An attorney can give a more realistic estimate after reviewing your file.

#### 2. Can I work while my asylum application is pending?

In many cases, yes, but there are waiting periods before you can apply for and receive a work permit, and the rules have changed over time. We confirm the current requirements when we prepare your case.

#### 3. What if I missed the one-year deadline?

You may still have options. Exceptions exist for changed or extraordinary circumstances, and withholding of removal or CAT protection don't carry the same deadline. A lawyer can review your history to see what's possible.

#### 4. Can my spouse and children be included?

Often yes. A spouse and unmarried children under 21 can usually be included in your application or petitioned for after approval, depending on the timing and your family's circumstances.

#### 5. Do I need a lawyer to apply for asylum?

The law doesn't require one, and the government doesn't provide one in immigration court. But the process is detailed and the consequences of mistakes are serious, so most applicants benefit greatly from experienced representation.

### Location-Related Questions

#### 6. Where will my asylum interview or hearing take place?

Affirmative cases are handled by a USCIS asylum office, and your notice lists the location. Court cases are heard in the immigration court named on your hearing notice. Detained cases are heard near the facility where a person is held.

#### 7. I live in Oakland or Daly City. Can you still help?
