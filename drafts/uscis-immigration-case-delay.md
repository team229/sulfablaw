# USCIS Case Outside Normal Processing Time: What Actually Moves a Stuck Case

**Quick answer:** Your case is late when your receipt date is past USCIS's posted case inquiry date. Then you escalate in order: e-Request, DHS Form 7001 to the CIS Ombudsman, a congressional inquiry, and finally a mandamus suit under 28 U.S.C. § 1361. Mandamus compels a decision, not an approval.

Most people jump straight to the lawsuit. That's a mistake, and an expensive one. The clients who get a decision fastest can prove on paper that the wait is costing them something specific: a job with a start date, an EAD running out, a child about to age out. A San Francisco [immigration lawyer](/immigration-lawyer-san-francisco/) can usually tell you which rung you're on in fifteen minutes.

## Why Your Case Sits for Months

USCIS doesn't run one queue. It runs dozens, and your case is in exactly one of them. That's why two people who filed the same form on the same day can be 14 months apart.

- A security check returns "pending" with no date attached. Nobody can tell you when it clears, because the answer isn't in the system.
- The file moves between a service center, the National Benefits Center, and a local office. The clock effectively restarts at each stop, so you're measuring against the wrong office.
- An RFE cycle. You respond in 30 days, then the case joins the back of a queue. Two rounds can add a year.
- No visa number. For an I-485 in a preference category, the Visa Bulletin controls, and no lawsuit moves your priority date.

"My case is stuck" usually translates to "my case is in a queue I didn't know existed." Find out which queue first.

## Find Your Real Case Inquiry Date First

Two official tools do the work, and both are free.

1. **Case status.** Put your receipt number into [uscis.gov/case-status](https://www.uscis.gov/case-status). "Case was received" tells you nothing about timing. "Case is at a local office" tells you the file moved.
2. **Processing times.** Open the [USCIS processing times tool](https://egov.uscis.gov/processing-times/), pick your form, category, and office, then read the "When can I ask about my case?" line. That's your case inquiry date, often later than the number quoted on forums.

Posted times are averages, not deadlines. USCIS says so itself. Past the posted date you've earned the right to ask a question, not the right to sue.

What makes a suit viable is a different statute. Under 5 U.S.C. § 555(b), "within a reasonable time, each agency shall proceed to conclude a matter presented to it." Congress added a number in 8 U.S.C. § 1571(b): the sense of Congress that a benefit application should be completed within 180 days of filing, and within 30 days for a nonimmigrant visa petition under § 1184(c). Courts treat it as a signal, not a stopwatch.

## Five Levers, Compared

Three of these are free. Read the cost column before you decide anything.

| Lever | What it actually does | Typical result | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| e-Request, "case outside normal processing time" | Creates a tracked service request against your receipt number | A written response, often a status confirmation. Occasionally it moves the file | Free |
| CIS Ombudsman, DHS Form 7001 | An independent DHS office reviews the file and pushes USCIS | A human reads it and can prompt action. It cannot order a decision | Free |
| Congressional inquiry | A district staffer calls USCIS's congressional liaison | Works with a documented hardship. Slow for routine delays | Free |
| Premium processing | Starts a statutory clock on eligible forms | A decision inside the clock, or a refund of the premium fee | Published fee per form |
| Mandamus and APA suit | A federal judge orders a decision by a set date | A decision within months of service. Not necessarily the one you want | Filing fee plus counsel |

None of the first four obliges anyone to decide your case. Only the fifth does.

## The Escalation Ladder, In Order

Work down the list. Each rung produces a document the next one asks for.

### Rung 1: Fix the obstruction you can fix yourself

Check four things first. Is your address current? A stale address is the most common cause of a case that looks dead but is waiting on a notice you never got. Did you answer every RFE by the deadline, with proof of mailing? Is your priority date current? Was biometrics completed?

We watched a case sit 11 months because a notice went to an old apartment. One form fixed it.

### Rung 2: e-Request, then a Tier 2 call

Go to [egov.uscis.gov/e-Request](https://egov.uscis.gov/e-Request/Intro.do), choose "case outside normal processing time," and enter your receipt number, receipt date, and form type. You'll get a reference number. Save it, screenshot the page, note the response window USCIS posts.

Then call 1-800-375-5283 and ask for a service request and a Tier 2 callback. Write down the date, time, and agent identifier. The Ombudsman, a congressional office, and your attorney will all ask for those numbers.

### Rung 3: Request an expedite

Expedites are free and decided on USCIS's published criteria: severe financial loss, an emergency, humanitarian need, nonprofit or cultural interest, government interest, or a clear USCIS error. Attach evidence, not adjectives.

Most are denied, and the Ombudsman won't take a case where USCIS just denied an expedite. Don't fire that request off the week before you ask for help.

### Rung 4: CIS Ombudsman, DHS Form 7001

The Ombudsman sits inside DHS but outside USCIS, under 6 U.S.C. § 272. It exists to help people who are stuck. It cannot compel USCIS to act, and it says so on its own site. The entry rules are stricter than most people expect:

- You must have contacted USCIS within the last 90 days *and* given the agency at least 60 days to fix it.
- Your case inquiry date must already have passed.
- If a congressional office inquired for you within the last 45 days, the Ombudsman won't take the case. Pick one, not both.
- If you're represented, include the signed Form G-28. Missing it gets the request closed.

File DHS Form 7001 through the portal at [dhs.gov/case-assistance](https://www.dhs.gov/case-assistance). Section 5 must name the petitioner or applicant having the difficulty, not the beneficiary and not the attorney. Get that wrong and the request is rejected unread.

### Rung 5: Congressional inquiry, or FOIA

Your representative or one of your senators can open an inquiry through a district office. You sign a privacy release, a caseworker calls the liaison, and a human gets on the phone. It works best with hardship on paper. Separately, 5 U.S.C. § 552 lets you FOIA your A-file. That won't speed anything up, but it shows whether a check is pending or a notice was issued that never reached you.

### Rung 6: Premium processing, where the form allows it

This is the fastest lawful way to force a clock. It covers four forms: the I-129, the I-140, certain I-765 categories, and certain I-539 classifications. It does *not* apply to the I-130 or the N-400, whatever a consultant tells you. The clock is 15 business days for most classifications, 30 business days for the I-765 and for an I-539 change of status to F, J, or M, and 45 business days for an I-140 multinational manager or national interest waiver. USCIS refunds the premium fee if it misses. Check the current fee on the [Form I-907 page](https://www.uscis.gov/i-907), because the agency adjusts it for inflation.

## What the Law Actually Says

Four provisions carry almost every delay case in federal court.

- **28 U.S.C. § 1331** gives the district court federal question jurisdiction. This is the door.
- **[28 U.S.C. § 1361](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/28/1361)**, the Mandamus Act, covers "any action in the nature of mandamus to compel an officer or employee of the United States or any agency thereof to perform a duty owed to the plaintiff." Read the last four words twice. The duty must be owed to you, and it must be nondiscretionary.
- **[5 U.S.C. § 555(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/555)** supplies the duty: the agency shall conclude a matter presented to it within a reasonable time.
- **5 U.S.C. § 706(1)** supplies the remedy: the court shall compel agency action unlawfully withheld or unreasonably delayed.

Then comes the case that kills sloppy delay suits. In *Norton v. Southern Utah Wilderness Alliance*, 542 U.S. 55 (2004), the Supreme Court held that a § 706(1) claim survives only where the plaintiff alleges an agency failed to take a discrete action it is legally required to take. You can sue to force a decision on your application. You cannot sue over how USCIS manages a backlog. Courts don't run agencies.

So a good mandamus complaint is narrow. It asks for one thing: a date certain.

## The TRAC Factors: How a Court Decides "Too Long"

No statute says 400 days is unreasonable and 300 is fine. Courts fill the gap with six factors from *TRAC v. FCC*, 750 F.2d 70 (D.C. Cir. 1984). The Ninth Circuit adopted them in *Independence Mining Co. v. Babbitt*, 105 F.3d 502 (9th Cir. 1997), so they govern a San Francisco filing.

1. **Rule of reason.** Is there a rational timetable, or is the agency simply not moving?
2. **Congressional timetable.** If Congress signaled how fast it expected action, that sets the benchmark. This is where § 1571(b) and its 180 days gets cited.
3. **Context.** Delays touching health and welfare trouble courts more than delays in economic regulation. Immigration sits in between, closer to welfare when family separation or an expiring status is involved.
4. **Competing priorities.** Would expediting your case push someone else back? This is the government's strongest argument, and why courts resist jumping anyone to the front of a queue Congress funded at a fixed size.
5. **Prejudice.** What is the delay doing to you? This is your strongest argument, and where most cases are won or lost.
6. **Impropriety.** You don't have to prove bad faith. Unreasonable delay alone can violate the APA.

Build factor five. "Documentation of consequences" means nothing to a judge. A job offer with a start date and an expiry is evidence. An EAD expiring in 40 days with no renewal receipt is evidence. So is a lease you can't sign, a tuition deposit you'll forfeit, or a child's age-out calculation under the Child Status Protection Act. Vague statements about stress are not.

Factor four explains why timing matters: suing on a case pending eight months, in a category USCIS processes in 14, is a good way to lose and pay for the privilege.

## If You Filed an N-400, You Have a Better Statute

Naturalization has its own remedy, and it's stronger than generic mandamus. Under 8 U.S.C. § 1447(b), if USCIS hasn't determined your N-400 within 120 days after the date of your examination, you may apply to the district court where you reside for a hearing. The court can decide the application itself or remand it with instructions.

That's the difference. In a mandamus case the court orders a decision and USCIS makes it. Under § 1447(b), the judge can decide.

One trap: the 120 days runs from the examination, not from filing. If USCIS never interviewed you, the clock has not started on the statute's terms, and you are back to the APA and the TRAC analysis. Where an interview was rescheduled or waived, courts have reached different results, so the date to anchor is the one on your interview notice, not your receipt notice.

## What Filing a Mandamus Suit Actually Looks Like

A delay suit is a short federal complaint, not a trial. The mechanics matter more than the rhetoric.

- **Where.** Under 28 U.S.C. § 1391(e)(1) you may file where a defendant resides, where the events occurred, or where the plaintiff resides. For a Bay Area client that usually means the Northern District of California.
- **Who.** Normally the Secretary of Homeland Security, the USCIS Director, and the district director responsible for your case. Naming the wrong office is a common error.
- **Service.** Rule 4(i) requires the U.S. Attorney for the district, including the civil process clerk, and the Attorney General. Miss it and the case sits while the clock runs.
- **The answer.** Rule 12(a)(2) gives the United States 60 days to respond after service. That's the first real date on the calendar.
- **The ask.** A writ under § 1361 and an order compelling action under § 706(1), with a deadline. Not an approval.

Usually the government adjudicates within weeks or months of service, the parties stipulate to dismissal, and the case closes without a ruling. Sometimes it moves to dismiss on jurisdiction. Sometimes USCIS remands and then denies. Plan for that third outcome.

A mandamus suit buys you a decision. It does not buy you a green card. If the decision is a denial, your options shift to motions, appeals, or a new filing, with short clocks on each. ## What This Costs

- **e-Request, expedite, DHS Form 7001, congressional inquiry:** no fee.
- **FOIA:** usually free or close to it. Non-commercial requesters are charged only for duplication, and you can ask for a public-interest fee waiver under 5 U.S.C. § 552(a)(4)(A)(iii).
- **Premium processing:** thousands, not hundreds, and it varies by form. Refunded if USCIS misses its clock.
- **Federal filing fee and service:** $405 for a civil complaint under 28 U.S.C. § 1914(a), which is the $350 filing fee plus the $55 Judicial Conference administrative fee, plus postage and service costs. The Judicial Conference adjusts these periodically, so confirm the number with the clerk before you file.
- **Attorney fees:** driven by how many plaintiffs are on the complaint, whether the government litigates, and where the case is filed. Ask for a flat fee and a written scope.

Fee shifting exists under the Equal Access to Justice Act, 28 U.S.C. § 2412(d). If you prevail and the government's position wasn't substantially justified, fees can be awarded at a statutory cap of $125 an hour, adjusted upward for cost of living, with the application due within 30 days of a final, non-appealable judgment. Two cautions: courts have split on whether a case mooted by a late approval makes you a prevailing party, and EAJA reimburses after the fact. Don't budget around it.

## The File You Need Before Anyone Can Help

Every rung runs on paper. Assemble this before your first consultation, because half of it takes weeks.

- **I-797 receipt notices** for every form filed, plus the petition or application as submitted.
- **Priority date** and current Visa Bulletin status. Print the bulletin page and date it.
- **Your EAD**, front and back, with the expiration date, plus any renewal receipt.
- **Every RFE, NOID, or NOIR** USCIS sent and every response you filed, with proof of mailing: certified mail receipt, courier tracking, or upload confirmation.
- **Every e-Request reference number**, with dates and any response. Screenshots, not memory.
- **Proof of harm.** The offer letter with a start date. The EAD expiration. The lease. The tuition bill. The medical record. The age-out calculation. This is the factor-five evidence, and it's what everyone forgets.
- **Address history** with dates, plus any AR-11 confirmations. And your **I-94, passport bio page, and prior approvals**, including expired ones.
- **Form G-28** if you're already represented.

Send copies. Never originals, never the only copy of anything.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How long is too long for USCIS to process an application?

There's no universal number. The benchmark is your own case: compare your receipt date to the case inquiry date for your exact form, category, and office. Past it you can ask. Well past it, with documented harm, is where a court starts listening.

### Can I sue USCIS if my application is delayed?

Yes, in the narrow sense that you can ask a federal judge to order a decision. Whether it survives turns on the TRAC factors and whether the duty is owed to you specifically. Filing is easy. Filing well is not.

### Will a mandamus lawsuit make USCIS approve my application?

No. Mandamus compels a decision, not an outcome. USCIS keeps full discretion over the merits and can deny you after the court order. What you buy is a date certain instead of an indefinite wait.

### Should I contact USCIS before hiring an attorney?

Yes, and keep the receipts. The e-Request reference number is a prerequisite for the Ombudsman and evidence later. Hiring a lawyer first means paying someone to do the free part.

### Can an immigration delay affect my work authorization?

It can, and this is the delay that breaks people's lives. Some EAD categories allow automatic extension while a timely renewal pends. Not all of them do. Check your category's rule and the date on your card.

### What if USCIS denies my application after a lawsuit?

The order bought you a decision, and it went against you. From there it's a motion to reopen or reconsider, an appeal, or a new filing, each with a short deadline. Whether the denial is a fixable error or a substantive problem comes from the denial notice.

### Where can I get official information about a delayed immigration case?

Three government sources: [USCIS case status](https://www.uscis.gov/case-status) for your receipt number, the [processing times tool](https://egov.uscis.gov/processing-times/) for your case inquiry date, and [dhs.gov/case-assistance](https://www.dhs.gov/case-assistance) for the Ombudsman's rules.
