balance What an Immigration Attorney in San Francisco Actually Does

Many people believe that hiring an immigration lawyer is just about filling out paperwork. The forms are the easy part. The real work of an immigration attorney is threefold:

  • First, read the file closely: Understand the client's full history, including immigration records, criminal records, entry dates, and prior filings, before the government does.
  • Second, choose the right forum and relief: Know whether the case belongs before USCIS, an immigration judge at 630 Sansome Street, the Board of Immigration Appeals, or a federal district court judge.
  • Third, stay on the case until there is a result: Immigration agencies are slow, unpredictable, and often nonresponsive. A lawyer who files and disappears is not doing the job.

Frequently Asked Questions

When should I hire an immigration attorney? ▼

As early as possible. If you have been arrested, received a Notice to Appear, are facing an interview, or are approaching an asylum deadline, waiting reduces your options. An attorney who gets involved early can shape the record before mistakes are made.

What is a habeas corpus petition in an immigration case? ▼

A petition for a writ of habeas corpus is a lawsuit filed in federal district court challenging the legality of someone's detention. It asks a federal judge to order the government to release the person or hold a proper bond hearing. It is used when ICE detains someone without statutory authority, holds them for an unreasonable length of time, or violates their due process rights.

Can someone in immigration detention get a bond hearing? ▼

In many cases, yes. Whether someone is eligible for bond depends on how they entered the country, whether they have certain criminal convictions, and what statute the government is holding them under. An attorney can evaluate whether mandatory detention applies and, if not, request a bond hearing before an immigration judge. Even where the government claims mandatory detention applies, that determination can sometimes be challenged in federal court.

How long does a family-based green card take? ▼

Timelines vary significantly depending on the relationship (immediate relative vs. preference category), the applicant's country of origin, and whether the process is handled through adjustment of status inside the U.S. or consular processing abroad. Immediate relative petitions (spouses, parents, and unmarried minor children of U.S. citizens) generally move faster because there is no visa backlog.

Is there a deadline to apply for asylum? ▼

Yes. In most cases, an asylum application must be filed within one year of your arrival in the United States. There are exceptions for changed circumstances or extraordinary circumstances, but they are narrow and must be carefully documented. If you are approaching that one-year mark, you should speak with an attorney immediately.

Do you take cases outside San Francisco? ▼

Yes. While our office is at 300 Montgomery Street in downtown San Francisco, we represent clients throughout the Bay Area, Northern California, and in federal courts across the entire state.

Which court will hear my case? ▼

Removal proceedings in the Bay Area are typically heard at the San Francisco Immigration Court, located at 630 Sansome Street. Habeas corpus petitions and mandamus actions are heard in federal district court — for Bay Area cases, the Northern District of California (with courthouses in San Francisco, Oakland, and San Jose).

What if my family member is detained far from the Bay Area? ▼

ICE frequently transfers detainees to facilities in the Central Valley (such as Mesa Verde or Golden State Annex) or Southern California. Because Maisoun is admitted in all four federal districts in California, she can file habeas petitions in the district where the detention facility is located.

Can I meet with an attorney if I cannot travel to the office? ▼

Yes. We offer consultations by phone and video conference for clients who cannot come to our Montgomery Street office, including family members of detained individuals.

Do you speak with clients from every Bay Area community? ▼

Yes. We regularly represent clients living in San Francisco, Oakland, Daly City, San Mateo, South San Francisco, San Bruno, Berkeley, Richmond, and surrounding communities. Language support is available.

Understanding Immigration Law in California

Immigration law is exclusively federal law, but its enforcement is felt deeply at the local level. In California, immigration matters intersect with state criminal laws, federal detention policy, and procedural protections under the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA).

Key forms of relief under the INA include:

  • Asylum (INA § 208): Protection for individuals with a well-founded fear of persecution based on protected grounds.
  • Withholding of Removal (INA § 241(b)(3)): Mandatory protection when life or freedom is threatened in the country of origin.
  • Convention Against Torture (CAT): Relief under federal regulations prohibiting removal to countries where torture is likely by or with the acquiescence of government officials.
  • Cancellation of Removal (INA § 240A): Available to both lawful permanent residents and non-permanent residents meeting statutory continuous presence and hardship thresholds.
  • Adjustment of Status (INA § 245): Converting temporary presence or uninspected entry (with waiver or statutory eligibility) into lawful permanent residence.
  • Habeas Corpus (28 U.S.C. § 2241): Article III federal court challenge to unconstitutional detention practices.
